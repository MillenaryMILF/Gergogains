The method

I'm an industrial engineer from Austria. My work is finding why a system produces less than it should, and fixing the one thing that actually limits it. These are the tools I use professionally. Every one of them has a direct, unforced translation to training — and together they are why this plan looks different from the ones you have followed before.

01

Theory of constraints

Bottleneck analysis

A production line runs at the speed of its slowest station. Everything upstream piles up, everything downstream waits. Adding capacity anywhere else changes nothing.

↓ applied to training

Your weakest muscle sets the ceiling for your whole upper body — and most plans give volume to whatever already feels strong.

02

Capacity planning

Load levelling

You cannot schedule more work into a shift than the shift can hold. Plans that ignore this do not fail loudly; they quietly under-deliver everywhere.

↓ applied to training

Four sessions carry roughly 150 counted sets. Ask for 200 and something silently gets cut. Here the targets are scaled to what your week can actually hold, and the shortfall is named.

03

Overall equipment effectiveness

Availability x performance x quality

Output is not one number. It is uptime multiplied by speed multiplied by how much of the output is usable. A line at 100% speed and 60% uptime is a bad line.

↓ applied to training

Adherence x intensity x execution. A perfect programme followed 60% of the time loses to an average one followed 95% of the time. That is why sessions here are capped at what you will finish.

04

Specification and tolerance

Range, not a single value

No engineer specifies a single number. They specify a target and the band around it where the part still works.

↓ applied to training

Twelve to sixteen sets, not 'twelve'. Training inside a band is what makes a plan survive a bad week.

05

Statistical process control

Signal versus noise

Every process varies. The skill is telling ordinary variation apart from a real shift that needs intervention.

↓ applied to training

One bad session is noise. Three weeks of falling performance at the same load is a signal — and that is what the scheduled deloads are placed to catch.

06

Root cause analysis

Five whys

You do not fix the symptom. You keep asking why until you reach the cause that, once removed, stops it recurring.

↓ applied to training

A muscle is not growing. Is it volume, proximity to failure, recovery, food, or execution? Adding sets to a technique problem just adds a technique problem.

07

PDCA cycle

Plan, do, check, act

Improvement is not continuous effort. It is bounded cycles: change one thing, run it, measure, decide, repeat.

↓ applied to training

An eight-week block with a deload is one cycle. Changing your programme every fortnight means you never reach the measuring step.

08

Measurement system analysis

Trust the gauge first

Before optimising anything you verify the instrument. An unreliable gauge makes every decision downstream worse than guessing.

↓ applied to training

Bodyweight swings two kilos on water alone. Weekly averages and a fixed measurement protocol come before any decision about your calories.

Questions, answered

What makes this different from every other plan generator?
Two things. First, sets are counted the way the research counts them: a set of incline press credits 1.0 to chest and 0.5 each to front delts and triceps, so your real weekly volume is visible instead of guessed. Second, your targets are checked against what your chosen number of sessions can arithmetically carry. If a target is unreachable, the plan says so and names the fix rather than quietly programming too little.
I'm a complete beginner. Is this for me?
Especially for you. Every plan scales: beginners get simpler movement selections, conservative loading and clear "2 reps in reserve" instructions. The structure stays identical as you advance — only the numbers grow. You won't outgrow it; you re-run it.
