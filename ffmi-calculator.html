What FFMI measures

BMI divides your total mass by your height squared, which means it cannot tell muscle from fat. Every lifter who has ever been told they are “overweight” at 12% body fat has met the limitation directly.

FFMI does the same division but throws the fat away first: it is your fat-free mass divided by height in metres squared. Two people at the same weight and height get very different FFMIs if one is carrying ten kilos more muscle.

Why the normalised figure exists

The raw index still drifts with height — taller people tend to score slightly lower for the same build, because mass does not scale to the square of height in a real body. The normalised figure corrects toward a 1.80 m reference so that two people of different heights can be compared at all. It is the number worth tracking.

The input that decides everything

Your FFMI is only as good as your body-fat estimate. Every part of this calculation except body fat is measured; body fat is guessed. A 4-point error in the estimate moves FFMI by roughly a full point, which is larger than a year of intermediate training.

Before optimising anything, verify the instrument. If you are tracking FFMI over time, use the same estimation method every time and compare the trend rather than the absolute value — a consistently wrong method still shows the direction correctly, whereas switching methods between measurements tells you nothing at all.

What the number cannot tell you

FFMI is a descriptive index, not a verdict. It says nothing about how that lean mass is distributed — two lifters at an identical FFMI can look entirely different if one has built their back and shoulders and the other has not. It is a gauge for total tissue, not for proportion, and proportion is what is actually visible.

You will find FFMI cited as evidence of a hard natural ceiling. That claim rests on a small and much-argued dataset, so it is not made here. What the number is genuinely good for is watching your own trend across months.

How this is calculated

  • Lean mass = bodyweight × (1 − body fat %)
  • FFMI = lean mass ÷ height in metres, squared
  • Normalised FFMI = FFMI + 6.1 × (1.8 − height in metres)
  • Protein = 1.6 to 2.2 g per kg of bodyweight per day
